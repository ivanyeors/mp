Before you read on, notice how you are sitting. Is your head drifting towards the screen, or is your back curving into the chair? Most of us slip into these positions without noticing, and by evening the neck and shoulders feel it.

This page covers practical posture habits, six simple exercises, how posture affects your breathing, and when to get help. It is part of our guide to the [best pillow for neck pain](/blog/what-is-the-best-pillow-for-neck-pain-in-singapore), which covers your sleep setup.

## Is there one correct posture?

No. There is little evidence for one ideal posture, and comfortable positions differ from person to person. What helps most is changing position often, rather than holding any posture for hours. [[cite: Slater et al. 2019 JOSPT "Sit up straight: time to re-evaluate" (NHS South Tees leaflet based on it): no strong evidence for one optimal posture; changing position helps | https://www.southtees.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Posture-Reformed-A5-4pp-V2.pdf]]

That said, long stretches slumped, or with your head pushed forward, are linked with sore necks, tight shoulders and headaches. [[cite: SingHealth HealthXchange: prolonged forward head tilt linked with neck soreness, shoulder tightness and headaches | https://www.healthxchange.sg/head-neck/neck-thyroid/text-neck-syndrome-symptoms-prevention]] The aim is a position you can relax in, and plenty of movement between positions.

## What daily habits improve posture?

Set up your desk so you do not have to reach or look down, and get up regularly. The same checks apply to your phone, your laptop and reading in bed.

### At your desk

- **Sit back in the chair** with your lower back supported. [[cite: NHS South Tees posture advice: sit with bottom at the back of the seat and lower back supported | https://www.southtees.nhs.uk/resources/posture-2/]]
- **Keep your feet flat** on the floor or on a footrest, with your knees about level with your hips. [[cite: NHS South Tees posture advice: feet flat, knees level with hips; SGH: use a footrest rather than letting feet dangle | https://www.southtees.nhs.uk/resources/posture-2/]]
- **Rest your forearms** with your elbows at about a right angle and close to your body. [[cite: SGH office ergonomics: elbows about 90 degrees and close to the body | https://www.sgh.com.sg/symptoms-treatments/office-ergonomics-occupational-overuse]]
- **Raise your screen** so the top sits at or slightly below eye level, about an arm's length away. [[cite: Mayo Clinic office ergonomics: top of monitor at or slightly below eye level, about arm's length away (prefer the mayoclinic.org original over this Care Network copy) | https://mymlc.com/health-information/articles/o/office-ergonomics-your-how-to-guide]]
- **Get up about every 30 minutes**, even for a minute. [[cite: SGH office ergonomics: break from sitting every 30 minutes | https://www.sgh.com.sg/symptoms-treatments/office-ergonomics-occupational-overuse]]

### On your phone and laptop

- **Bring the screen up** rather than bending your neck to look down at it. [[cite: Mayo Clinic neck pain prevention: hold small screens up rather than bending the neck | https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/neck-pain/symptoms-causes/syc-20375581]] Our page on [what tech neck is and how to ease it](/blog/what-is-tech-neck-and-how-can-a-pillow-help-fix-it-in-singapore) goes further.
- **Give a laptop a stand** and use a separate keyboard and mouse, so the screen can sit higher. [[cite: Mayo Clinic office ergonomics: laptop with external keyboard, mouse and stand | https://mymlc.com/health-information/articles/o/office-ergonomics-your-how-to-guide]]

| Setting | Quick check | Quick change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Office desk | Is the top of the screen near eye level? | Raise the monitor on a stand |
| Laptop | Are you looking down at the screen? | Use a stand and a separate keyboard |
| Phone | Is your chin close to your chest? | Bring the phone up towards eye level |
| Long meetings or calls | Have you been still for 30 minutes? | Stand, walk or stretch for a minute |
| Reading in bed | Is your head propped on stacked pillows? | Sit up with support behind your back |

## Which exercises help posture?

Simple mobility and strengthening exercises for the neck, shoulders and upper back keep the muscles that hold you upright working. For long-lasting neck pain, strengthening and endurance exercise for the neck and shoulder area has the best research support. [[cite: Gross et al. 2015 Cochrane review: moderate-quality evidence for strengthening and endurance exercise in chronic neck pain | https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD004250.pub5/information]]

Move slowly and stay within comfort. A mild stretch is fine, but stop if an exercise causes sharp or increasing pain. [[cite: NHS neck range-of-motion advice sheet: mild ache acceptable, severe pain means stop | https://northcheshireandmersey.nhs.uk/media/emlfdohl/neck-range-of-motion-exercises-patient-advice-sheet.pdf]] If you have a diagnosed neck or back condition, ask a physiotherapist for a programme made for you.

Most of these can be done at your desk or during a short break. The doorway stretch needs a door frame, and the last one needs a mat, a rug or your bed.

### Chin tuck

Sit tall and look straight ahead. Draw your chin straight back, as if making a double chin, without tipping your head up or down. Hold for a few seconds, relax, and repeat about 10 times. [[cite: NHS Lothian neck pain exercises: chin tuck - pull chin backwards without moving head up or down | https://services.nhslothian.scot/musculoskeletal/neck-pain/]]

### Neck turns and tilts

Keep your shoulders relaxed and slowly turn your head to look over one shoulder, then the other. Next, tilt your ear towards your shoulder on each side, without lifting the shoulder to meet it. [[cite: NHS Lothian neck pain exercises: head turn and head tilt with shoulders relaxed | https://services.nhslothian.scot/musculoskeletal/neck-pain/]]

### Shoulder rolls

Roll your shoulders up, back and down in a slow circle, then reverse the direction. Repeat 5 to 10 times each way. [[cite: NHS Fife neck pain leaflet: shoulder rolls in both directions, 5 to 10 times | https://www.nhsfife.org/media/33106/neck_pain_v7.pdf]]

### Shoulder blade squeeze

Sit or stand tall with your arms by your sides. Gently draw your shoulder blades back and down towards each other, pause, then relax. [[cite: Berkshire Healthcare NHS thoracic back pain leaflet: draw shoulder blades gently down and back | https://www.berkshirehealthcare.nhs.uk/media/168318/thoracic_back_pain_leaflet.pdf]]

### Doorway chest stretch

Stand in a doorway with your forearm against the frame and your elbow at shoulder height. Step forward with the leg nearest the frame until you feel a gentle stretch across the front of your chest. Hold, then swap sides. [[cite: NHS South Tees anterior shoulder stretch: forearm on door frame at shoulder height, step through until a gentle chest stretch | https://www.southtees.nhs.uk/resources/anterior-shoulder-stretch/]]

### Face-down shoulder blade lift

Lie on your front with your elbows bent at right angles beside you. Squeeze your shoulder blades together and lift your forearms just off the floor. Hold briefly, then lower. [[cite: Cambridge University Hospitals scapula position exercises: prone, elbows at right angles, lift forearms while squeezing shoulder blades | https://www.cuh.nhs.uk/patient-information/scapula-position/]]

## How does posture affect your breathing?

Slumping, or pushing your head forward, leaves your chest less room to expand. In small studies, people recorded lower lung-function readings in these positions than when sitting upright. [[cite: Koseki et al. 2019, J Phys Ther Sci: forward head posture reduced FVC, FEV1 and peak flow in 15 healthy men | https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6348172/]] [[cite: smartphone-use posture associated with lower FVC and FEV1 | https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4905897/]]

You can feel the difference yourself. Sit slumped with your head forward and take a slow, deep breath. Then sit tall, let your shoulders drop, and breathe in again. Notice whether the second breath feels different.

The same idea applies at night. A pillow so high that it pushes your head forward bends your neck in much the way slouching does. [[cite: Harvard Health: a pillow too large behind the neck pushes the head forward | https://www.health.harvard.edu/pain/say-good-night-to-neck-pain]]

Posture is not the answer to every breathing problem at night. Loud snoring, pauses in breathing, gasping, or waking exhausted can be signs of sleep apnoea, so see a doctor. [[cite: NHS sleep apnoea: main symptoms; see a GP | https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/sleep-apnoea/]] Our page on [pillows for CPAP users](/blog/how-a-customised-pillow-can-help-with-sleep-apnea-for-cpap-users) explains the limited role a pillow plays.

## Does your sleep position affect your posture?

Your sleep position decides how your neck and back are supported for hours, though it will not change how you stand during the day. Side and back sleeping are the easiest positions for the neck. [[cite: Harvard Health: side and back are the two positions easiest on the neck | https://www.health.harvard.edu/pain/say-good-night-to-neck-pain]]

- **On your side:** use a pillow high enough for your shoulder width. A pillow between your knees helps line up your spine, pelvis and hips. [[cite: Mayo Clinic sleeping positions for back pain: pillow between the knees for side sleepers | https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/back-pain/in-depth/sleeping-positions/art-20546852]]
- **On your back:** use a lower pillow with support under your neck, and try a pillow under your knees. [[cite: Mayo Clinic sleeping positions for back pain: pillow under the knees for back sleepers | https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/back-pain/in-depth/sleeping-positions/art-20546852]]
- **On your front:** this is the hardest position for the neck, and HealthHub Singapore advises people with neck pain to avoid it. [[cite: HealthHub Singapore neck pain: avoid sleeping on your stomach | https://www.healthhub.sg/health-conditions/neck-pain]]

## When should you see a doctor or physiotherapist?

If posture-related neck or back pain has not improved after about six weeks of self-help, see a healthcare professional. [[cite: NHS Lothian/NHS inform: if neck pain has not improved within 6 weeks, speak to a healthcare professional | https://services.nhslothian.scot/musculoskeletal/neck-pain/]]

Get help sooner if the pain spreads into your arms or legs, or comes with numbness, tingling or weakness. The same applies to pain that follows a fall or injury. [[cite: Mayo Clinic neck pain: when to see a doctor (radiating pain, numbness, tingling, weakness; injury) | https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/neck-pain/symptoms-causes/syc-20375581]]

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best sitting position for long hours?

There is no single best position. Sit back with your lower back supported and your screen near eye level, and change position or stand up about every 30 minutes.

### How often should I do posture exercises?

A few repetitions of each exercise, several times a day, is a practical starting point. Desk breaks are an easy time to fit them in.

### Can bad posture cause headaches?

Headaches are among the symptoms commonly linked to long periods with the head tilted forward. A headache that is new, severe or persistent should be checked by a doctor.

### Does slouching affect breathing?

Small studies show lower lung-function readings when people slump or push their head forward than when they sit upright. Sitting tall gives your chest more room to expand.

### Can a pillow improve my posture?

A pillow cannot change how you sit or stand during the day. The right height keeps your neck in line with your spine while you sleep, so the night does not add to the strain.
